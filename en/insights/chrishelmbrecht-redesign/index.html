Seven days, one skull, three games. My new website – and what it says about AI.

chrishelmbrecht.com is new: one drawing as a homepage, hidden games, clocks showing real Munich time. Built alone with AI in seven days. By hand: three to four months and a team of five.

  • AI
  • Web & usability

My personal site chrishelmbrecht.com is new. Not freshened up – redrawn, rebuilt, rethought. It is deliberately more than a business card needs to be. It is a showcase: this is how much is possible today when one experienced person and a handful of AI tools get seven days.

Seven full days. Alone. No designer, no developer, no illustrator, no agency. More on that in a minute – first the site.

A skull as a homepage

The whole site is one drawing. A skull in blue ink, like Delft porcelain, and inside it the cities of my life: Moscow, Munich, Tenerife, New York. An owl, the moon, a path with two hikers setting off. Every part is a door. The moon leads to the DJ mixes, the owl to the bio, the trees to the blog, the Twin Towers to the press page, the teeth to contact. A click flies you in, and on the way back you fly out through the same door.

The drawing is alive. A flock of swallows circles in the dome, the water moves, the skull tilts toward your cursor – on a phone it follows the hand that holds it. And after three minutes of nothing, everything goes quiet. A phone should not get hot from a skull.

The small things

This is the part I enjoyed most.

The clocks on the Frauenkirche show real Munich time. On the homepage and in the bio. Nobody ordered that. It is there anyway.

The helicopter in the bio never turns around. A mirrored helicopter looks wrong – so it flies out one side and comes back in on the other.

On the DJ page, the tonearm of the turntable sits exactly where the recording is right now. Lift it, drop it somewhere else, and the music jumps with it.

Shake the branch three times and the owl flies away. She comes back.

The hidden parts

There are three games hidden on the site, and a few things you only find on the second visit. I am not giving directions here – the site does that itself, in one place, for people who would rather not hunt.

One I have to tell you about, because it is the closest to me: the flying lesson. You sit in an Alouette II and try to hold it steady over a meadow while the instructor shouts at you. I learned to fly on exactly this machine, in the helicopter squadron of the German Federal Police. That was my first lesson. Now it can be yours. Three levels, from Easy to Realistic – and “Realistic” means it. The Alps below you are not painted. They are computed from real elevation data. The mountain you see is actually there.

The other two: an arcade game the early eighties would have built, with a high-score table and three initials like a real arcade. And an avenue in Manhattan, sometime between 1997 and 2002, six lanes, a lot of yellow cabs, and a light that says DONT WALK and never changes.

That is all I will say. Go look.

The question everyone asks

How long would this have taken by hand?

I have been building websites since 1995, so I can do the rough math. An illustration of this quality plus six animations living inside it: two to three weeks for a good illustrator and a motion designer. The homepage with navigation, fly-in effect, tilt and power-saving logic: two weeks of front-end work. Three games, one of them with a flight model and real terrain data: one to two weeks each. The 360° viewer, the turntable control, the bio with map and swallow: another two weeks. Two languages, SEO, accessibility, copy, testing on real devices: two weeks.

Three to four months. A team of four or five. A mid five-figure sum, if you are honest about it.

It took me seven days. Not because the AI spits out a website at the push of a button – it does not, and anyone who claims otherwise has never tried. But because I knew what I wanted, how to build it, where it would break and when a draft was good enough. The tools drew, animated, voiced and coded. I decided. Every round.

And there were many rounds. The hiker who lost his ornaments after four seconds. The helicopter that looked stupid mirrored. The dots on the doors that got thrown out again. Half the work is restraint.

Under the hood

No WordPress. No page builder, no framework, no theme, no thirty plugins. Hand-written HTML, CSS and JavaScript, for this site and nothing else.

The result: the homepage is there in a quarter of a second. The HTML weighs three kilobytes. The films load afterwards, and only if your connection can take them – on data-saver mode the drawing stays still and never loads them at all. For comparison: an average WordPress site drags two to four megabytes and fifty requests before you can read the first sentence.

No cookies, no tracking, not a single third-party script. The only outside service is the Mixcloud player on the DJ page, and it loads when you press play, not before.

Built to be found – by Google and by AI assistants: every page carries its description and structured data, there is a sitemap, and an llms.txt tells ChatGPT, Claude and Perplexity what is where.

Built to work for everyone: doors and hidden spots are real links and buttons with names, reachable by keyboard. If you have “reduce motion” set on your device, you get a calm drawing. Without JavaScript, every text stays readable. On a phone the skull follows your hand, and the flying lesson works with a thumb.

WordPress is dead. Not because it is bad. Because the reason you needed it – “I can’t code, so I need a system that does it for me” – no longer holds. I maintain a site like this today in conversation with my AI. New blog post, new photo, new page: describe it, check it, done. Faster than any backend, and no plugin update will ever take the site down.

What comes next

The site is not finished, and that is on purpose. Next up is a timeline with photos from thirty years – New York, Tenerife, Moscow, Munich. Plus more gadgets and details I am not going to spoil here.

Until then: go, click on things that do not look clickable. Shake the branch. Find the helicopter. And if you crack the high-score table, let me know.

And if you want something like this

Not necessarily a skull. But a site that loads in a quarter of a second, tracks nobody, gets found by AI assistants and that you can maintain yourself – in conversation, without a backend, without an agency for every text change.
